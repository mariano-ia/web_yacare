Build or Buy an AI Solution

Before you spend on development, it pays to know when an off the shelf tool is enough and when you need something custom. A simple framework for deciding build vs buy in AI.

M
Mia Torres June 15, 2026 7 min read

The dilemma every founder faces

The question of building or buying software is old, but AI made it more confusing. Today there are dozens of ready made tools that promise to solve almost any problem, and at the same time it has never been so accessible to build something of your own. That abundance of options complicates the decision instead of simplifying it.

The good news is that this is not an ideological choice. It is a concrete calculation: how central that capability is to your business, how much each path costs, and what happens when the context changes. This article gives you a framework to answer those three questions without falling for the hype.

Let's be honest from the start: the right answer is almost always to buy first and build later, if needed at all. But there are clear exceptions and they are worth understanding.

This is not an ideological choice. It is a concrete calculation about how central that capability is to your business.

When buying an off the shelf tool makes sense

Buying makes sense when your problem is common and the market has already solved it. If you need to transcribe meetings, generate text, classify emails, or handle frequent inquiries, there is most likely a mature tool that is cheaper and more reliable than anything you could put together in three months.

The main advantage is speed. You can be operating in days, not quarters, and pay a monthly subscription instead of funding a development team. You also transfer maintenance, model updates, and security to the provider, who has more scale than you to sustain it.

Before committing to a platform, it pays to understand where to start with automation and review the common mistakes when applying AI in a company. Many frustrations come from buying the wrong tool for a poorly defined problem.

When a custom solution makes sense

Building makes sense when the AI capability is part of your competitive advantage, not an operational cost. If your product lives or dies by the quality of its recommendation engine, that engine cannot be the same one your competitors get by buying the same API.

A custom AI solution also makes sense when your data is unique and hard to integrate with generic tools, or when you have privacy and compliance requirements that standard platforms do not cover. In those cases, control is worth what it costs.

The mistake is building out of technical pride or fear of depending on a provider. Before taking that path, it pays to read how much it costs to build a product with AI and think about going from prototype to MVP. Building is not buying freedom, it is taking on an ongoing responsibility.

The hidden costs of each option

Buying seems cheap until you add up integrations, per user licenses that grow with your team, and provider dependence. If the tool raises prices, changes its model, or shuts down, your operation is exposed. The cost of migrating later is usually greater than the initial savings.

Building seems controlled until the maintenance costs appear, along with monitoring models that degrade over time and the salary of the team that has to sustain it. An AI project does not end when it launches: it begins there. That is the part almost no one budgets for.

An underrated middle path is to buy the foundation and build only the layer that sets you apart. You use a pretrained foundation model and add your data, your rules, and your interface. That way you avoid reinventing the common parts and focus your effort where it really matters.

How to decide in practice

Start by separating the core from the accessory. Make a list of the AI capabilities your business needs and mark which ones are differentiators and which are commodities. Commodities you buy without hesitation; differentiators deserve the build debate.

Then estimate the total cost over two years, not just the initial one. Include integration, maintenance, provider risk, and the opportunity cost of your team. Often the two year number completely changes a decision that seemed obvious in the first month.

If you are not sure, don't invest blindly. A Discovery Sprint lets you validate the idea and walk away with a clear plan in a week, before committing budget. You can also validate an idea with AI without code to test the hypothesis with real customers before choosing a path.

The build or buy decision is not forever. Start by buying to learn fast, and build when you have evidence that the capability deserves to be yours. The worst option is to decide in the abstract, with no data and without having tested anything.

Frequently asked questions

Is buying an AI tool cheaper than building one?

In the short term, almost always yes, because you avoid development and team costs. But over two or three years the calculation changes based on the number of users, integrations, and provider dependence. It pays to estimate the total cost, not just the initial price.

When does an AI capability justify a custom build?

When it is part of your competitive advantage and not just an operational cost. If the quality of that capability determines whether you win or lose against competitors, you cannot depend on the same tool everyone else uses. If it is a commodity, buy it.

Can I start by buying and build later?

Yes, and it is usually the smartest strategy. Buying lets you learn fast with low risk, and you build only when you have evidence that the capability deserves its own investment. The key is to choose tools that won't trap you if you later want to migrate.

How do I avoid buying the wrong tool?

Define the problem well before looking at solutions and test with a small pilot instead of committing upfront. A discovery process or a no code validation helps you confirm that the tool solves what you need before you pay.
